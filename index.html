About

I'm an environmental scientist with a NERC-funded PhD in Environmental Engineering from Imperial College London (2025). My doctoral research focused on process-based modelling of coupled hydrological, carbon and vegetation dynamics under climate change, at the intersection of eco-hydrology and Earth system science.

Before my PhD, I taught Geography at Cheltenham Ladies' College. I have since built applied experience across local government, NGOs, the private sector, the UN and CGIAR. My professional work includes large-scale agricultural climate impact modelling and the development of operational decision-support tools.

Water for rice

Hydrology, irrigation and water-use optimisation in rice systems, using process-based models, field data and AI.

Overview

Rice is one of the world's most water-intensive crops. As a postdoctoral researcher at the International Rice Research Institute (IRRI), part of CGIAR, I work on water management and hydrology in rice systems, with a focus on optimising irrigation and water use.

The work combines process-based hydrological modelling with AI methods to support decisions on when and how much to irrigate. Water management in paddies is closely linked to methane emissions, and fieldwork includes emissions measurement, reporting and verification (MRV).

This page will bring together maps, dashboards and outputs from that work as they become available.

Cheltenham Ladies' College

Teaching at Cheltenham Ladies' College remains a highlight of my career. At 22, freshly out of university and with the ink on my teaching qualification barely dry, I joined one of the UK's most prestigious schools. The students were unforgettable: driven, proactive and eager to learn every single day.

As a Teacher of Geography I taught students aged 11 to 18. Highlights included setting up a Geography Society, field trips to Wales, mock Oxbridge interviews and a fantastic day-to-day life. It remains one of the proudest and most memorable chapters of my life, thanks largely to the amazing staff and students at CLC.

Alongside my role at CLC, I completed a Master's in Education at the University of Cambridge, specialising in cross-curricular learning. My research explored how integrating overlapping themes between science and geography can strengthen interdisciplinary links and improve the student learning experience.

A Vespa through the Swiss and Italian Alps

125ccyellow Vespa, “Bumblebee”
40 km/hclimbing
~90 km/hdescending

Taking a yellow 125cc Vespa across Switzerland from east to west, over some of the highest alpine passes along the way, is no small feat. The bike is designed for city life rather than alpine touring, yet it proved a remarkably reliable companion.

Bumblebee and I would climb slowly through the Alps, then fly back down the descents. The most unsettling part wasn't the terrain but the lack of protective gear: little more than a helmet. One of the most nerve-racking experiences was plunging into long, dark tunnels at speed: the sudden temperature drop, the disorienting transition, and those few seconds of total darkness before my eyes adjusted.

I alternated between a one-person tent and, when it made sense, the occasional hotel splurge. The coldest night I've ever experienced was in St. Moritz; I thought I might lose feeling in my extremities. It's no surprise the town is home to the world's only natural-ice bobsleigh and skeleton track.

From there I swept through Italy, where the Vespa blended in more naturally. People often smiled as I passed, and occasionally someone would wave me down for a photo. Those moments usually turned into brief exchanges in whatever shared language we could manage, often a mix of English and Spanish that eventually collapsed into my broken Italian.

Lake Iseo stood out as the most beautiful of the Italian lakes, especially compared to Lecco, which was overflowing with tourists and had gelato queues stretching over 100 metres. Aosta was similarly busy, particularly its medieval main street.

The road back home was straight and dull.
